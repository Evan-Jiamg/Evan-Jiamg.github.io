<h2 id="sdc">SDC Software Development Club, NYCU · Aug 2025 – Aug 2026</h2>

<div class="t-sub"><i>Treasurer (財務)</i></div>

As Treasurer of the NYCU Software Development Club (SDC), I was the club's financial contact with the assistants of ITSA, a Ministry of Education talent program that funds the club, and I handled the reimbursement of every ITSA grant. I changed three things from the previous term:

1. **I filed all 44 reimbursement claims myself, about NT$130,000 in total.** They covered events such as a hands-on workshop on the system boot process (QEMU, U-Boot and Linux), an SRE Workshop and SDC Intern Corner, as well as officers' meeting meals and equipment grants.
2. **I started a weekly finance announcement.** The previous term had kept few detailed financial records, so I put the finance side of my major to work: after each weekly finance meeting, I posted the budgets approved that week, the receipts received, and the items ITSA had already paid to the Financial Announcement channel on Discord. Every payment had a public record that anyone in the club could check.
3. **I handed over with nothing missing.** At the previous handover, some receipts had not been submitted to ITSA on time. During my term, every receipt was submitted on time, and the club's cash was fully accounted for when I handed over.

This experience taught me that the heart of treasury work is not bookkeeping but a process everyone can see. When the status of every payment is public, anyone can check on progress at any time, and a handover no longer depends on one person's memory, because every flow of money has a clear record to match against.

<h2 id="sitcon-2026">SITCON 2026 · Mar 2026</h2>

<div class="t-sub"><i>Students' Information Technology Conference (學生計算機年會) · Venue Team (場務組) · Academia Sinica</i></div>

Through SDC members, I learned that there is a technology conference organized entirely by students. As a member of the Venue Team, I helped with attendee check-in, on-site support, catering and logistics. Working on the organizing side, I saw for the first time that behind a smoothly run event and live stream there is a very united organizing team, and I came to understand the ongoing discussion and inspiration that drive the SITCON community.

<h2 id="nepal-2026">International Volunteering in Nepal · Jan 2026</h2>

<div class="t-sub"><i>VYA Vision Youth Action (願景青年行動網協會) · Nepal NL89 volunteer team</i></div>

In January 2026, I joined the NL89 volunteer team of VYA Vision Youth Action and spent 10 days at a mountain school in Nepal, teaching students aged 10 to 18 in English. I was responsible for the character education and gender education classes for the senior students.

Once I was in the classroom, I realized I could not simply follow the lesson plans we had prepared. I first had to understand the students' circumstances, and only then decide how to teach:

1. **Unsure of their direction → getting to know themselves.** Many students, even at sixteen or seventeen and about to leave for high school in Kathmandu (the school system there differs from Taiwan's), still had no idea where they wanted to go in life. So I built my character education class around "knowing yourself," guiding them to think about their interests and future careers.
2. **A conservative culture → short skits.** Nepali culture is fairly conservative, and even more so in the mountains, so gender topics are rarely discussed openly. Together with other volunteers, I used short skits to show the boundaries between boys and girls during adolescence, which made the class interactive rather than a lecture at the whiteboard.
3. **Too shy to speak → singing together.** The students were warm and unpretentious, but often too shy in front of volunteers from Taiwan, and classes tended to start in silence. To break the ice, I began every class by leading the senior students in singing "Be What You Wanna Be." It helped everyone relax, and because the lyrics mention many different jobs, I also hoped the song would show them how many opportunities and possibilities their futures hold.

This experience showed me that learning does not come only from textbooks: stepping into a different culture and connecting with people from different backgrounds is a form of learning in itself. It also taught me that good teaching is not delivering prepared material word for word, but first understanding where the other person stands and then adapting how you teach.
